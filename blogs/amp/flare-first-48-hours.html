The First 48 Hours of an IBD Flare: A Calm Checklist

Posted on June 13, 2026 · Wellness

Content note: Educational content aligned with publicly available patient materials from the Crohn's & Colitis Foundation and other major IBD education sources. Not individual medical advice.

Educational use only. IBDPal does not provide medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Always consult your gastroenterologist or IBD care team for personal decisions.

Flares feel urgent even when they are not emergencies. A short plan lowers chaos.

Hour 0-12

  • Contact your IBD team per their after-hours instructions if fever, heavy bleeding, or severe pain
  • Sip electrolyte fluids; avoid alcohol
  • Eat gentle foods your clinician has previously OK'd
  • Log symptoms in IBDPal

Hour 12-48

Rest, cancel nonessential obligations, refill medications if low, and gather data for a triage call: stool frequency, pain level, temperature, weight change.

Call Promptly If

Significant blood, dehydration signs, rigid abdomen, or fever over clinician thresholds. When in doubt, use your clinic's on-call line.

Hour zero: pause and assess calmly

The first hours of a suspected flare feel urgent, but rash decisions add stress. Note when symptoms changed, what you ate, whether you missed doses, and any fever or bleeding.

Start gentle hydration with water, oral rehydration solutions, or electrolyte drinks if diarrhea is frequent. Avoid large fatty meals that may worsen cramping.

Rest if you can. Sleep loss amplifies pain perception and makes it harder to follow your clinician's plan the next day.

What to log before you call the clinic

Record stool frequency, blood or mucus, abdominal pain score, temperature, and weight if you have a home scale. Photos of stool are optional; written descriptions usually suffice.

List all medications taken in the last week, including steroids, biologics, NSAIDs, and supplements. Missing even one biologic dose can matter.

If you use a symptom app or paper diary, export or photograph entries so nursing staff can review trends quickly.

Medication steps unless told otherwise

Do not stop prescribed IBD medications without speaking to your GI team unless you were given explicit stop rules for infections or surgery.

If you have a written flare action plan with rescue steroids or dose changes, follow it exactly. If unsure, call before improvising.

Avoid NSAIDs like ibuprofen unless your clinician approves an alternative plan. Acetaminophen may be safer for fever or pain in many IBD patients.

When to escalate within 48 hours

Call promptly for heavy bleeding, severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, signs of dehydration, or fever. Your team may direct you to urgent care, infusion center, or emergency department.

Children, older adults, pregnant patients, and post-operative patients may need lower thresholds for outreach. Follow pediatric or surgical instructions first.

If phone lines are closed, use the on-call provider rather than waiting two days with worsening bleeding or pain.

Food and rest in the first two days

Bland, low-residue meals may reduce mechanical irritation while you await clinician guidance.

Avoid aggressive exercise during acute symptoms; gentle walking and stretching are usually enough.

Children and older adults dehydrate faster and need lower thresholds for outreach.

Preparing for the clinic callback

Have pharmacy phone numbers and insurance card photos ready before nursing calls return.

Note whether symptoms differ from prior flares in location, blood amount, or fever.

Ask about stool tests for infection if antibiotics or travel preceded the flare.

Planning ahead when life gets busy

Pack medications, snacks, and a small symptom kit before exams, trips, or overtime weeks when routines slip first.

Identify backup clinicians or infusion centers near work, campus, or relatives in case flares occur away from home.

Discuss preventive plans with your clinician before predictable stress seasons such as finals, tax season, or postpartum return to work.

When symptoms shift despite good habits

Return to your GI team if new bleeding, fever, weight loss, or pain appears even when you follow general lifestyle guidance.

Labs and stool markers sometimes change before you feel improvement, and sometimes lag behind symptoms. Your clinician interprets both together.

Do not assume setbacks mean personal failure. Inflammatory bowel disease activity fluctuates and often responds to timely medical adjustment.

Keep pharmacy and infusion center numbers saved in your phone before flares start, when decision-making is hardest.

A small go-bag with wipes, spare clothes, and copies of your medication list helps if you must leave home during urgent symptoms.

Common questions

Should I start a clear liquid diet immediately?

Some patients feel better on bland or low-fiber foods during early flares, but infants, underweight patients, and diabetics need personalized advice. Call your team before strict restriction.

Can I wait until my scheduled visit next month?

Worsening symptoms over 48 hours usually warrant earlier contact. Early treatment may shorten flares and reduce complications.

Is it a flare if calprotectin was normal last month?

Yes, labs can lag symptoms. Clinical worsening still deserves assessment even when recent tests looked stable.
