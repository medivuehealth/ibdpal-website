Nutrition snapshot: Honey and sweeteners

Honey is mostly fructose and glucose. Stevia and monk fruit are non-nutritive sweeteners. Sugar alcohols (sorbitol, mannitol, xylitol, maltitol) in sugar-free products commonly cause gas, bloating, or diarrhea.

Approximate macros (typical serving)

  • Honey serving: 1 tablespoon (~21 g)
  • Calories (honey): ~60
  • Carbohydrate (honey): ~17 g
  • Stevia / monk fruit: Negligible calories in pure forms
  • Sugar-free candy: Often high in sugar alcohols; GI effects vary

Amounts vary by brand, ripeness, cooking method, and portion. Use a label or USDA FoodData Central for exact numbers, and IBDPal food logs to compare your portion to how you felt afterward.

Micronutrients and extras people ask about

  • Fructose load: Large honey servings can be high FODMAP for some
  • Trace minerals in honey: Not a meaningful micronutrient strategy
  • Additive blends: Stevia packets may include erythritol or fillers

Flare versus remission: how many people approach honey and sweeteners

Small amounts of honey in tea or cooking are different from large spoonfuls or sugar-free candy binges. If you follow a low-FODMAP trial, honey is often limited. Stevia is frequently better tolerated than polyols, but blends matter.

During a flare (when your team wants gentler textures)

  • Skip sugar-free gums and candies with sorbitol or mannitol if diarrhea is active
  • Use tiny honey amounts only if your team allows simple sugars
  • Prefer plain stevia or monk fruit without polyol blends when testing
  • Watch 'natural' syrups stacked on already sweet drinks

In remission (when variety is usually easier)

  • Reintroduce honey in teaspoon amounts and log symptoms
  • Read labels on protein bars and 'keto' desserts for sugar alcohols
  • Keep total added sugar aligned with your dietitian's plan

If you have strictures, recent surgery, or a short bowel history, fiber, skins, seeds, and chunky textures may need a different plan. Follow your clinician, not a generic list.

Prep ideas that often feel kinder

Stir a teaspoon of honey into warm tea rather than eating it by the spoon. For baking, test recipes with half the usual sweetener and note stool changes for a day.

Common myths about honey and sweeteners and IBD

  • Honey heals the gut lining. It is a sugar source, not disease-modifying therapy.
  • All artificial sweeteners are identical. Polyols often differ from stevia or aspartame in GI effects.
  • Stevia is unsafe for every IBD patient. Many people use it; individual tolerance and product blends still matter.

How to track honey and sweeteners with IBDPal

Log the form (fresh vs canned, cooked texture, sweetener type, brand), portion size, and symptoms for 24 to 48 hours. Patterns beat memory at clinic visits. See tracking food and symptoms.

Questions for your gastroenterologist or dietitian

  • Are honey or polyols restricted on my current diet plan?
  • Which sweetener blends fit my FODMAP or diarrhea pattern?
  • How should I read labels on sugar-free snacks?

Bring your food log and recent labs so advice matches disease location, medicines, and nutrition gaps.

When food questions become urgent

Contact your care team promptly for severe pain, vomiting, inability to keep fluids down, heavy bleeding, fever, or rapid weight loss. See flare help and when to go to the ER.
