IBD Medication Monitoring Checklist: Labs, Screens, and Follow-Up Questions

Posted on November 21, 2026 · Treatment

Content note: Educational content aligned with publicly available patient materials from the Crohn's & Colitis Foundation and other major IBD education sources. Content is used under license from the Foundation for patient education; the Foundation does not endorse IBDPal or MediVue products or services. Last reviewed August 2026. Not individual medical advice.

Educational use only. IBDPal does not provide medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Always consult your gastroenterologist or IBD care team for personal decisions.

People looking for an IBD medication monitoring checklist want a practical list of screens and labs that often appear before and during treatment. Exact schedules belong to your gastroenterologist. This page helps you ask complete questions and track what was already done.

Pair this with vaccines on immunosuppressants, TDM, and Foundation medication guide.

Before starting many advanced therapies

  • Infection history and travel exposures
  • Tuberculosis screening when indicated
  • Hepatitis B and sometimes hepatitis C testing
  • Vaccine status review (live versus non-live timing)
  • Baseline blood counts, liver enzymes, and kidney function as ordered
  • Pregnancy plans and contraception conversations when relevant

While on therapy: themes by medicine class

Different medicines need different rhythms. Thiopurines, methotrexate, JAK inhibitors, S1P modulators, anti-TNF agents, and other biologics each have their own labels and clinic protocols. Ask for a written monitoring calendar instead of memorizing internet lists.

  • Blood counts and chemistries: common for several immunosuppressants
  • Lipids or other specialty labs: may apply to some small molecules
  • Drug levels / antibodies: see TDM for selected biologics
  • Skin and infection vigilance: report fevers, cough, shingles symptoms, or new skin lesions promptly

Build your personal checklist

  • Drug name, dose, and next due date
  • Last TB / hepatitis screens and results
  • Last vaccine dates
  • Upcoming lab dates and who reviews them
  • Portal message plan if a lab is abnormal

Questions for clinic and pharmacy

  • Which labs are required at baseline, week 4 to 12, then ongoing?
  • Who calls me if a result needs action?
  • Which vaccines should I complete before immunosuppression intensifies?
  • What symptoms should trigger holding a dose and calling?

When to seek prompt care

Fever with immunosuppression, severe headache with neurologic symptoms, chest pain, uncontrolled bleeding, or rapidly worsening abdominal pain need urgent evaluation. A checklist does not replace triage. See flare help.

Education supports shared decisions. It does not replace your gastroenterologist or pharmacist. Keep one shared note that caregivers and teens can both update.

If labs are drawn at an outside facility, confirm the clinic receives results. Missing faxed labs are a common delay.

Traveling? Ask how to time labs and infusions across time zones before tickets are booked.

Why monitoring checklists prevent quiet harm

Many IBD medicines reduce immune activity or change immune signaling. That can be life-changing for inflammation and still require structured safety watching. Problems are often silent at first: blood count changes, liver enzyme rises, lipid shifts for some medicines, infection risk, or delayed vaccine protection. A checklist turns vague advice into dated tasks you can verify in the portal.

Monitoring is also how teams catch secondary issues early, such as steroid side effects during bridges, iron deficiency during ongoing blood loss, or nutrient gaps after surgery. Your checklist can include disease monitoring and medicine safety monitoring in one place so nothing depends on memory alone.

Baseline screens before advanced therapy

Before many biologics or small molecules, clinicians review infection risk. Tuberculosis screening methods vary by history and local practice. Hepatitis B testing matters because reactivation risk exists with some immunosuppressive strategies. Additional viral testing may be ordered based on risk. Vaccine review should happen early because live vaccines may be timed differently once immunosuppression intensifies.

Baseline labs often include blood counts and comprehensive metabolic panels. Some medicines need specialty baselines such as lipid panels. Pregnancy testing may be appropriate before teratogenic medicines. Eye history can matter for selected older therapies. Dermatologic history matters if skin cancer risk counseling is part of the plan. Ask for a written list of what was already completed versus still pending so prior-authorization timing does not outrun safety steps.

Ongoing labs: ask for cadence, not folklore

Internet schedules are often wrong for your exact medicine and dose. Ask your clinic for cadence in writing: weekly, biweekly, monthly, then spaced out. Clarify whether local lab chains are acceptable and whether you must use a health-system lab for protocol consistency. Ask who reviews results within how many business days. If you travel for work, ask how to keep the cadence without missing doses.

For selected biologics, therapeutic drug monitoring joins the checklist. For immunomodulators, metabolite monitoring may be used in some practices. For JAK inhibitors and other small molecules, label-based labs and infection vigilance are essential. Your pharmacist can help reconcile the label with the clinic protocol when instructions seem to conflict.

Vaccines, dental care, and procedures

Put vaccine dates on the same checklist as labs. Inactivated vaccines are often encouraged, but timing around therapy starts can matter. Live vaccines need individualized decisions. Dental procedures, colonoscopies, and surgeries may need medicine-timing plans. Write the plan down before the procedure day so on-call staff are not guessing.

If you are due for cervical cancer screening, skin checks, or bone density testing because of steroid history, add those preventive items. Medicine monitoring is broader than a single CBC.

Infection and symptom watch list

  • Fever, productive cough, or shortness of breath
  • Shingles-like rash pain
  • Severe headache with vision changes or neurologic symptoms
  • Unexplained bruising or bleeding
  • Dark urine, jaundice, or intense right-upper abdominal pain
  • Signs of dehydration during flares while on therapy

Ask which symptoms mean hold the dose and call today versus message the portal. Holding decisions should come from your care team, especially if stopping abruptly is risky in your situation.

Caregiver and teen versions of the checklist

Families do better with one shared note that lists drug name, specialty pharmacy phone, next lab date, next infusion or injection date, and emergency contacts. Teens transitioning to adult care should practice requesting their own lab results and explaining their medicine list. Bring the checklist to every specialty visit, including dermatology and primary care, so duplicate immunosuppressants or interacting medicines are caught.

Bottom line for patients

An IBD medication monitoring checklist is a living document: baseline screens, ongoing labs, vaccines, procedure timing, and symptom triggers. Ask for cadence in writing, confirm who reviews results, and keep dose dates honest. Education helps you organize. Your gastroenterologist and pharmacist set the medical rules for your exact therapy.

Clinic script you can copy

"Please give me a written monitoring checklist for my medicine: baseline screens already done, upcoming labs, vaccine plan, and symptoms that mean I should call before the next dose."
